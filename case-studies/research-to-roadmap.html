Research & strategy · Case study

From signals to roadmap

How mixed-method research replaced competing opinions with a prioritized plan for a complex, multi-audience product.

Context

The product served several distinct audiences with overlapping but not identical jobs-to-be-done. Roadmap conversations had become a contest of loudest stakeholder or most recent support ticket. Leadership needed a defensible way to decide what to build next — and what to stop pretending would happen “soon.”

The goal was not more research for its own sake, but better decisions: fewer dead-end bets and more confidence in where we invested design and engineering time.

My role

I led the research design and synthesis, partnered with product on workshop facilitation, and worked with design and analytics to connect qualitative insight to usage data. I was accountable for a transparent method the org could repeat, not a one-off deck.

Approach

Align on decisions, not “themes.” We started by writing down the exact decisions the roadmap had to support (e.g., which audience to optimize for in the next two quarters, which workflow to simplify first). That kept the research effort proportionate and avoidable scope creep in check.

Triangulate. We ran structured interviews and usability sessions with representatives from each segment, paired with behavioral data — funnels, task completion proxies, and search/support signals — so stories had anchors in reality.

Make tradeoffs explicit. Synthesis used lightweight frameworks the team already trusted (impact × feasibility × confidence, with accessibility and risk called out). Findings were expressed as opportunities with evidence, not as persona posters that age out in a month.

Outcome

Leadership adopted a single prioritized backlog tied to evidence. Teams cancelled two initiatives that had looked strategic on slides but failed against real user behavior, and replanned a third around a workflow that research showed was the primary failure point. Design and engineering gained a shared narrative they could reference in critique and sprint planning — which cut repetitive debates and rework.

The repeatable playbook — decision framing, mixed methods, synthesis templates — became the foundation for the next planning cycle so research did not restart from zero.

What I would tune next time

Earlier involvement from customer-facing teams (support, success) to shorten the loop between qualitative themes and operational metrics — and a tighter SLA for how quickly synthesized findings feed backlog grooming.
